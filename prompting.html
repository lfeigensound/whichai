How to ask

Four habits. Then real examples.

Say what you want,
what it's for,
and what would make it wrong.

The four habits

1

Give it the job, not the topic

"Tell me about EESSH2" is a topic. "Which EESSH2 milestones fall in the next 18 months, and which of our stock types are at risk" is a job.

2

Say who it's for

A board, a client, a colleague, or yourself. This changes length, tone and how much it explains. It cannot guess.

3

Give it permission to say no

Add: "If you can't verify something, say so rather than filling the gap." This one line prevents most invented regulations.

4

Paste the source in

Don't ask what a regulation says — paste it and ask what it means. Night-and-day difference in accuracy.

Real examples

✗ Too vague

What are the ESG requirements for social housing?

No jurisdiction, no timeframe, no purpose. You'll get a generic, mostly English, possibly outdated summary.

✓ Same question, working

I've pasted the EESSH2 guidance below. Which milestones fall before December 2027, and what would a mid-size Scottish RSL need to have in place for each? For a board audience — one page, table format. Scottish law only; flag anything that's England-and-Wales. If a date isn't in the text I've given you, say so rather than filling it in. [paste guidance]

Source given, jurisdiction fenced, audience named, format set, permission to say "I don't know".

✗ Asking it to remember what it can't

What did the consultation I mentioned earlier say about retrofit funding?

In a new chat it has no idea. Even in a long chat, early detail gets lost. Never assume it's holding something.

✓ Re-paste, always

Here's the consultation response again [paste]. Compare its retrofit funding position against the draft position in our paper [paste]. Where do they conflict? Quote the specific lines.

Both sources present. "Quote the specific lines" forces it to point at real text rather than paraphrasing from nothing.

✗ Fishing for agreement

I think we're compliant with SHQS on this — does that sound right to you?

You've told it the answer you want. Both models lean towards agreeing. You'll get validation, not a check.

✓ Asking to be challenged

Here's our SHQS compliance position [paste]. Argue the opposite. Where is this weakest? What would a regulator or a sceptical auditor push back on first? Don't soften it.

Now it's looking for holes instead of confirming you. This is the single highest-value prompt pattern in your work.

✗ Everything at once

Research the new Scottish retrofit regs, check our stock data against them, and write a board paper.

Three jobs. It'll do all three badly and you won't be able to tell which part went wrong.

✓ One job per message

Message 1 (Gemini): find and cite the current regs Message 2 (Claude): here they are — what applies to us? Message 3 (Claude): now draft the board paper from that

Each step checkable before the next. When something's wrong you know exactly where.

Phrases worth keeping

"If you can't verify it, say so." — stops invented detail

"Scottish law only. Flag anything England-and-Wales." — stops the most common error in your field

"Quote the specific line." — forces it back to the source

"Argue the opposite." — breaks agreeableness

"What am I not asking that I should be?" — surfaces the gap you didn't see

"Shorter." — works, immediately, every time
